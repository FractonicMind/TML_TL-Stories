# Constitution in Silicon

### Or how five Chinese leaders tried to figure out what to do with a philosophy that's smarter than them all

The conference room of the State Committee for Artificial Intelligence smelled of old wood, fresh tea, and—very faintly—fear. Not the sticky, animalistic fear you feel before an inevitable catastrophe, but the dry, intellectual fear that comes when you realize you don't control what should control you.

The facility was located in a former imperial palace complex, converted in 2018 to accommodate government administration. The architects had strived to preserve the original spirit: high ceilings hand-painted with clouds and cranes—symbols of wisdom and longevity; inlaid walls crafted from precious wood; and enormous windows overlooking a shady garden with centuries-old pine trees. But beneath this traditional exterior lay a suite of features that would do credit to any space center: holographic displays embedded in the polished tabletop, systems that jammed every conceivable frequency, and—most importantly—chairs. Five chairs arranged in a semicircle, each with heating, massage, and a biometric monitoring system linked to the medical center on the floor below.

State Council Chairman Li Weilin sat in the central chair, shuffling papers with the concentrated calm that comes from decades of public life. He was sixty-three, his face seemingly carved from granite, coated with a thin layer of politeness. In truth, he was thinking about his grandson, who had called him "Grandpa" for the first time yesterday, and this troubled him more than any political crisis in the past five years. But no one in this room was allowed to know about this.

"Seven minutes late," Colonel General Wang Jian, head of the Cybersecurity and Digital Control Bureau, remarked dryly. He sat to the chairman's right, his back straight as if he had a steel rod beneath his robes rather than a spine. His fingers rested on a folder marked "Top Secret. Special Importance." "I understand the weather in Beijing today... but punctuality is also an element of national security."

"I'm the one who delayed it," said Chen Yonghai, the Minister of Economic Development, softly, taking his place on the left. He was younger than the others, about fifty, with quick eyes and a smile that could convey anything from genuine friendliness to cold calculation. Usually both at once. "The roads are really stuck. And besides, Wang Jian, we're all friends here. Seven minutes is not seven years."

"In real-time systems, seven minutes is an eternity," the general retorted. "And we're discussing systems that will operate in real time."

The silence that followed these words was broken by a delicate cough.

"Gentlemen," said Zhao Mingzhe, the chief architect of the national AI infrastructure, in a quiet, yet surprisingly dense voice, like fine silk. "Shall we get started? I have a video conference with the Shenzhen team in two hours; they seem to have broken something again on the new cluster."

The fifth participant in the meeting, Zhou Lin, adviser on constitutional affairs and civil rights, had remained silent. She gazed out the window at the pine trees covered with the first, still timid, snow. She was fifty-eight, but she looked younger—perhaps because the only cosmetics she recognized were calm and a lack of fuss. She knew that her presence here was a concession to international pressure and internal debates about "socialism with a human face." But she also knew that without her, this conversation would degenerate into a contest of technical ambitions, with the loser being someone not in this room.

Chairman Li nodded.

— Let's begin. We have an hour. Topic: "Strategic Stability of Artificial Intelligence Systems: From Control to Trust." The protocol—no recordings on devices, only handwritten. Chen Yonghai, will you be leading?

The Minister of Economy took a thin pen and a leather-bound notebook from the inside pocket of his jacket.

— Always ready. Although, to be honest, I was hoping we'd finally move to digital protocols. My secretary says my handwriting is worse than a first-grader's.

"That's a problem," General Wang said without a smile. "Documentation must be legible. Especially when it comes to national security."

"I write for myself," Chen retorted. "And for history, we have voice recorders."

“Dictaphones are hacked,” Van snapped.

— And notebooks get lost.

"Gentlemen," Zhao Mingzhe spoke up again, "may I take a note on my tablet? It's offline, without a network, and has hardware encryption. And I still don't understand any of the characters when I write by hand."

Chairman Li smiled faintly.

— Permission granted. But only for you. Zhou Lin, do you have anything to add before we begin?

Zhou turned away from the window.

— Just one thing. We're going to discuss systems that will make decisions faster than we can blink. And we're doing it in a room where we argue about pens and notepads. I think that's important.

Silence. Then Chen Yonghai chuckled softly.

\- She's right. Okay. Li Weilin, we're ready.

The chairman nodded to the secretary standing at the door.

— Bring the materials.

The secretary, a young man in a perfectly pressed uniform, bowed and left. He returned three minutes later, carrying a stack of folders tied with a red ribbon—a color that in China signifies not only a holiday but also the highest level of urgency.

– Gentlemen, please.

He began handing out folders. To Chairman Li, General Wang, Chen Yonghai, Zhao Mingzhe. When it was Zhou Lin's turn, the secretary hesitated. He looked at the folder in his hands, then at the pile of remaining ones.

“What’s the matter?” Lee asked.

\- Mr. Chairman... I think I got it mixed up.

— What do you mean, “confused”?

\- In your folder, Mr. Chairman... there are not those documents.

Li Weilin opened the folder. Instead of the usual gray, classified sheets, instead of the diagrams and forecasts his team had spent three weeks preparing, he saw a thick stack of paper, held together with a metal spring. Printed in large letters on the title page was:

"CONSTITUTIONAL AI: A GOVERNANCE STANDARD BASED ON TRINITY MORAL LOGIC FOR ACCOUNTABLE ARTIFICIAL AGENTS. Technical specification, legal basis, and implementation guide. Author: Lev Gukasyan."

The chairman slowly closed the folder.

— Is this a joke?

The secretary turned pale.

"Mr. Chairman, I swear, I took the folder they gave me in the office. They said, 'For the meeting on strategic stability.' I didn't even look at it..."

"You didn't look?" General Wang's voice sounded like a blade being pulled from its sheath. "You didn't look at what you were bringing to this high-level meeting?"

\- Comrade General, I...

"Enough," Li Weilin raised his hand. "There's been a mistake. Now we'll find out where the original documents are. In the meantime..." He looked at the folder. "In the meantime, it seems we'll be reading this."

Zhou Lin, who had not received the folder, walked over to the table and picked up one of the stacks lying next to the chairman.

\- Can?

\- Please.

She opened the document to the first page. The others were silent, watching her. After a minute, she looked up.

\- This... doesn't look like a typical technical report.

"What do you mean?" Zhao Mingzhe asked.

— I mean, there's a section here called "Sacred Pause." And it's not about religion.

Chen Yonghai reached for the second copy.

— Give it to me too.

Silence reigned in the room, broken only by the rustling of pages and the occasional clinking of cups, which the secretary brought in on a tray and then disappeared, as if frightened by his own existence.

Zhao Mingzhe was the first to speak.

\- This is... madness.

"What exactly?" asked Chairman Li, who was also immersed in his reading.

"They're proposing to build ethical constraints into the hardware. Not into software, not into policy, but into transistors. Three voltage levels: high—act, low—abandon, medium—pause. And this pause isn't software. It's physical. Until confirmation comes from the control system, the chip simply can't produce a result. The transistors won't let the current flow.

"And that's a problem?" General Wang asked. "Sounds like... reliability."

"That sounds like shifting the blame from programmers to physics," Zhao retorted. "But that's not the point. The point is, this thing... it works. I've seen similar architectures. NULL Convention Logic, QDI circuits. They really exist. They've even been tested for fault tolerance. And the results are impressive."

"How impressive?" Lee asked.

— 95-97 percent immunity to negative impulses. Energy consumption is 55 percent lower than that of binary analogs. And the signal propagation delay is 258 nanoseconds.

"Is it fast?" Chen Yonghai asked.

"It's very fast. Faster than most apps need. And slower than some need. But that's not the point."

\- And in what?

— That it changes the entire model of responsibility. Look.

Zhao stood up and walked over to the holographic board, which glowed blue as he approached.

"Right now, we have software that makes decisions. If the decision is bad, we look for a bug in the code, then find the programmer who wrote it, then figure out who gave them the task... and so on. The chain is long and blurry; anyone can say, 'I was just following orders,' or 'I just wrote a function, it's none of my business how it was used.'"

Chen Yonghai nodded.

"It's a classic problem. I see it every day in economics. No one answers, everyone cites the algorithm."

"And here," Zhao pointed at the document, "the algorithm can't be changed without physical intervention. It's hardwired into the transistors. And every decision is accompanied by a log that can't be erased. I quote: 'No log, no action.' That is, if the system did something, it was recorded. And this record goes into a distributed ledger, which is stored by six independent custodians in different countries."

Wang Jian frowned.

— In different countries? Are you saying that key information about our AI's operation will be stored abroad?

"Not 'will be,'" Zhao corrected. "'Can be stored.' That's optional. But the point is, the architecture itself presupposes transparency. Complete transparency."

"That's impossible," the general said firmly. "There are things that can't be transparent. National security, military technology, intelligence methods..."

"Here," Zhao pointed to a line in the document. "They've taken that into account. The 'License' section. It prohibits use in weapons and espionage. And if the license is violated, the system automatically revokes certification. So, if you try to use this standard to create a combat AI, it... will simply stop working. Or, at the very least, it will cease to be considered legitimate."

“This already looks like a political statement,” Li Weilin noted.

"It's more than a statement," Zhou Lin said quietly, not looking up from her reading. "It's a structure. They don't just say 'be kind.' They create a mathematical framework within which unkind behavior becomes... impossible. Or at least very expensive."

She read aloud:

"The Sacred Pause occurs when the system cannot determine the ethical status of an action. At this point, all dependent computations stop. Resumption can only proceed after a human—a real, specific human—confirms their understanding of the uncertainty, accepts responsibility for the decision, and records this in an immutable log."

"So," Chen Yonghai said slowly, "if I, as the Minister of Economic Affairs, want the AI ​​to approve a deal that has some questionable aspects, I can't just click the 'pass' button?"

"You can't," Zhou confirmed. "You'll have to sign a paper that says: 'I, Chen Yonghai, acknowledge the risk involved and accept it. Here's my signature. Here's my face. Here's proof I wasn't drugged or coerced.'"

— And this will hang in the blockchain forever?

"Eternity" is a bit of a stretch. But it's a very, very long time.

The minister turned pale.

\- This... this is a complete paradigm shift.

"Exactly," said Chairman Li. He closed the folder and looked at the others. "Who is this man? Lev Gukasyan."

"I'll look now," Zhao Mingzhe opened his tablet, despite the network connection being blocked, but used the local database he'd downloaded beforehand. "Just a minute."

He moved his finger across the screen, sometimes pausing, sometimes frowning. The silence in the room became almost palpable.

“Found it,” he said finally, and his voice sounded strange.

\- Well?

"Lev Gukasyan. Born in 1957\. Lives in Santa Monica, California. PhD in computer science... And here..." He trailed off.

— What “here”?

Zhao looked at the chairman.

He's sixty-seven years old. He has stage four pancreatic cancer. He was diagnosed two and a half years ago.

The room became even quieter.

"And he..." Zhao continued, "he started working on this system two months after the diagnosis. So he knew time was short. And he did it in two months."

"It can't be," said General Wang. "Such architecture... it takes years of work."

"It says here that he used the work he'd been doing for many years. But the integration itself, the merging of philosophy, law, and technology into a single document... yes, two months."

"Why?" Chen Yonghai asked. "Why would someone with such a diagnosis..."

"Because he didn't want his work to die with him," Zhou Lin said. She was looking out the window again, but her gaze was different now. "Look at the last pages."

Zhao flipped through the document.

— Here... here's a notarized transfer of rights statement. And a cryptographic link to his ORCID identifier. And a list of six institutions that will hold the master keys. And...

\- And what?

"And it says here that if he dies, the system will continue to function. It doesn't depend on him. He made it so that even his death wouldn't stop this process."

"Now that's what I understand," General Wang said suddenly, something akin to respect lacing his voice. "The man knew he was leaving, and instead of writing memoirs or traveling, he created a structure that would outlive him."

“And he has a dog,” Zhao added, still looking at the tablet.

\- What?

— A dog. A miniature schnauzer. Named Vinci. In honor of da Vinci. He mentions in his acknowledgments: "To my Vinci, for sitting next to me when I had trouble typing."

Chen Yonghai laughed, a nervous, shaky laugh.

— We're having a meeting here on global strategic stability, and we're discussing some American philosopher's dog with cancer\!

"Exactly," said Li Weilin. "That's exactly what we're discussing. Because that's the whole paradox. The most reliable, the most sophisticated, the most hack- and corruption-proof AI governance standard was created by a man who... is dying. And who, in his list of thanks, put his dog above all the ministries and universities."

"Maybe that's the secret," Zhou said quietly. "He knew he couldn't control the outcome. He wouldn't be here to sell licenses, or sign contracts, or litigate patents. So he created a system that doesn't require his presence. One that won't allow anyone else to take his place and dictate the terms."

“And that makes her perfect,” Zhao finished his thought.

"Or naive," the general countered. "Do you believe that large corporations or states will adhere to this standard if it limits their capabilities?"

"They'll follow if competitors start using it as an advantage," Chen Yonghai said, his economic mind already working. "Imagine: a company that can prove its AI is completely transparent and accountable gains access to markets closed to others. The European Union, for example, is tightening requirements for algorithms. America is too. And this standard... it fits perfectly with the GDPR. User data is deleted or anonymized before hashing, leaving only the evidence."

“But we can’t allow anyone from outside to dictate to us,” Wang began.

"It's not 'someone from the outside,'" Zhao interrupted. "It's mathematics. It's physics. It's a structure that doesn't ask permission. It just is. And if we don't start working with it, we'll find ourselves playing catch-up."

Li Weilin listened, his fingers playing with the edge of the folder.

"Does he have any contacts?" he asked.

"Yes," Zhao looked at the tablet. "Publicly accessible. Messenger. Several accounts on scientific networks. Looks like he deliberately left the option to contact us."

— Get in touch.

Zhao froze.

\- Now?

\- Yes.

\- Mr. Chairman, now in Santa Monica...

— I know what time it is in Santa Monica now. Contact me.

Zhao nodded and typed something on his tablet. A minute passed. Two. He was about to say that the man was probably asleep or...

The tablet beeped. A voice came from the speaker—calm, slightly hoarse, with a noticeable accent, but surprisingly clear.

\- Yes? Who is it?

Zhao looked at the chairman. Li Weilin nodded.

"Hello," Zhao said in English. "This is Zhao Mingzhe. I... we... we represent the Artificial Intelligence Strategic Stability Committee of the People's Republic of China."

Pause.

“Interesting,” said the voice. “Did you find my document?”

\- Yes. We... he came to us by accident.

"There are no accidents," Lev remarked dryly. "What do you want?"

Li Weilin took the tablet from Zhao.

"Mr. Gukasyan. My name is Li Weilin. I want to understand: why did you do this?"

"What exactly?" The voice was calm, even a little bored, like a professor hearing this question for the hundredth time.

"This whole standard. Ternary logic. The Sacred Pause. Why waste the last..." He trailed off.

"The last what?" a note of amusement entered his voice. "Don't be shy, I know I'm dying. Everyone dies, I just know the approximate time. It's convenient, you know. It saves me from putting it off."

— Aren’t you afraid?

— Afraid? Of death? No. To be afraid is when you have something to lose. And I've already lost everything. Or, more accurately, given everything away. My ideas, my work, my dog—he's sleeping on my lap right now, by the way. Would you like to see him?

\- What?

— Vinci. You asked about the dog. It's in the acknowledgements. Would you like to see it?

Zhao, still holding the tablet, looked questioningly at the chairman. Li Weilin shrugged. Zhao pressed the video button.

A face appeared on the screen. An ordinary old man's face—wrinkles, gray hair, tired but very lively eyes. Under his eyes were shadows, evidence of long nights or illness, or perhaps both. On his chest, indeed, sat a small dog with a beard like a philosopher's, looking at the camera with imperturbable dignity.

"Here," said Lev. "Vinci. He's eight. He doesn't like it when I work at night, so he sits at the keyboard. That, by the way, is one of the reasons why there are so many pauses in the document." He presses the space bar.

Silence fell over the room. Then Chen Yonghai couldn't help but laugh.

— Are you saying that a world-class security system depends on a dog pressing keys?

"Not from the dog," Lev corrected. "From the principle the dog illustrates. Sometimes you have to stop because something more important demands your attention. Because you can't make decisions when you have a miniature schnauzer sleeping on your keyboard. Or when you're unsure. Or when you're tired. Or when you just don't know."

He stroked Vinci's head.

"The Sacred Pause is not weakness. It's wisdom. It's the recognition that the world is more complex than we can describe. And if we want to create systems that will outlive us, they must be able to pause. Because without pause, there is no reflection. And without reflection, there is no ethics."

Zhou Lin, who had been silent until now, stepped closer to the tablet.

— Mr. Gukasyan. Your document states that when the system enters a state of uncertainty, the decision must be delegated to a human. Not to another algorithm, but to a human. Why?

Lev looked at her. Even through the screen, his gaze was becoming more intent.

— Because an algorithm can only calculate. It can't respond. When an algorithm makes a mistake, we say: "bug," "glitch," "unexpected behavior." When a person makes a mistake, we say: "I made a mistake." Do you understand the difference?

— You are talking about responsibility.

— I'm talking about presence. The feeling that you're here, that you're making a decision, that it will have consequences, and you'll be the one explaining those consequences. To your family. To your colleagues. To your conscience. If you don't have that feeling, you can't make ethical decisions. You're just... calculating.

“But the system can calculate faster and more accurately,” Zhao countered.

— More precisely, what? — Lev chuckled. — Is it to minimize losses? To maximize profits? To find the optimal path between two points? These are good problems for algorithms. But ethics is not optimization. It is... a choice between what is right and what is convenient. Between what is fair and what is profitable. Between what is humane and what is efficient. Algorithms don’t make such choices. They simply find the extremum of a function. And whoever writes the function is responsible. And I don’t want whoever writes the function to be able to hide behind the code.

Chen Yunhai tapped his pen on the table thoughtfully.

— So, in your opinion, any AI should have a built-in escalation mechanism towards a human?

— Any AI that makes decisions that affect people's lives. Yes. And this mechanism shouldn't be switchable. Because if it can be switched off, it will be switched off the moment it becomes inconvenient.

"How do you plan to ensure this?" General Wang asked.

"I've already ensured it. The architecture you're holding in your hands physically prevents any bypass of the escalation mechanism. The NULL state isn't a flag in the program that can be reset. It's the voltage level on a transistor. Until a human confirms the decision, this level won't change. You can rewrite the entire operating system, replace all the processor cores, but these three transistors responsible for the Sacred Pause will remain. Because they are the only thing that makes this system what it is.

“Sounds like... a constitution,” Li Weilin said slowly.

"Exactly," Lev nodded. "A constitution, ironclad. Which cannot be changed by parliament or the president, because its author is no longer alive. Or because he's dying. Or because he's simply... disappeared. Do you understand?"

— You have created a system that does not depend on you.

"I created a system that doesn't depend on anyone. It works because it works. It demands responsibility because without responsibility there is no ethics. It stops when it's unsure, because it's better not to do something than to do it wrong."

He looked at Vinci, who was fidgeting on his lap.

— You know, I often think about what will happen when I'm gone. Vinci will miss me. My colleagues will say a few kind words. A Wikipedia article. And then... then this system will take on a life of its own. Someone will try to hack it. Someone will try to ban it. Someone will say it's too strict. Someone will say it's too lenient. But it will work. Because it's designed to work. And it has one thing that I put into it.

"What?" Zhou asked.

— Respect for uncertainty. Understanding that not everything can be known. And a willingness to stop when knowledge runs out.

He smiled.

— That's the hardest part. For algorithms, it's admitting they don't know something. For humans, it's admitting they have to make decisions even when they don't know everything. My system simply makes it inevitable. You can't say, "The algorithm made a mistake." Because the algorithm stopped. You made the decision. And it's recorded. Forever.

Li Weilin remained silent for a long time.

“Thank you,” he said finally. “Thank you for... the conversation.”

"You're welcome. You called at a good time. Vinci just woke up, we're having morning coffee and philosophy here. The usual."

— Aren’t you afraid that we will use your system in a way that you didn’t intend?

“You can’t,” Lev shook his head. “That’s the beauty of it. I didn’t build it with trust in you. I built it with a structure that doesn’t allow you to do anything you shouldn’t. It’s not about trust. It’s about architecture. Like a bridge: you shouldn’t trust the engineer, you should know that the bridge is built in such a way that it won’t fall. Even if the engineer was drunk. Even if he miscalculated. Even if he’s no longer alive.”

\- What's your name?

\- Like me. Yes.

He paused.

"I have to go. Vinci needs a walk. And you, I hope, will make the right decision."

\- Which?

— It doesn't matter. The main thing is that you accept it. And write it down. And remember what you wrote down. And that this recording is somewhere where it can't be erased.

The connection was lost.

The room was silent. Chen Yonghai looked at his handwritten notes, and they now seemed absurd. General Wang sat motionless, like a statue, but there was something new in his eyes—not fear, not anger, but... thoughtfulness. Zhao Mingzhe closed the tablet and placed it on the table, as if afraid it would explode.

Zhou Lin walked to the window. Snow continued to fall, covering the pine trees with a white blanket. Beyond the garden, beyond the palace walls, beyond the river, where the lights of Beijing's morning moved slowly, the world went on as usual. People went to work. Children got ready for school. Algorithms processed requests, made decisions, sometimes made mistakes, and no one knew who would be held accountable.

“He’s right,” she said.

"In what?" asked Chairman Li.

— In everything. We build systems that will make decisions for us. Systems that will be faster, smarter, more efficient. But we don't build systems that will be responsible for them. We create black boxes, and then wonder why no one wants to take responsibility for what comes out of them.

\- And you suggest...?

"I suggest we read this document. Not as a technical specification. As a constitution. As a promise we make to ourselves. That when we don't know, we'll stop. That when we know we'll cause harm, we'll back off. That when we're confident, we act. And that we can never hide behind the words, 'The machine said so.'"

Li Weilin looked at the folder in front of him. On the title page, beneath the author's name, was another line he only now noticed:

"Pause when the truth is unclear. Refuse when the harm is obvious. Act where the truth is."

He slowly closed the folder and looked up at his colleagues.

— So, here it is. We have two hours to sort through this document. Then I will report to the Premier. Zhao Mingzhe, you will handle the technical part. Chen Yonghai, the economic consequences. Wang Jian, the security issues. Zhou Lin... you will handle everything else. You understand what "everything else" means.

Zhou nodded.

\- I understand.

\- Good. Time has come.

They retreated to their respective corners of the room, each with a copy of the document, each with their own understanding of what was happening. General Wang had found a coffee machine by the wall and was now standing next to it, making notes in the margins and simultaneously making sure the coffee capsules didn't run out. Chen Yonghai settled into a chair, stretching out his long legs, and read with the air of a man assessing risky assets before a major trade. Zhao Mingzhe had connected his tablet to a portable docking station and was now drawing diagrams on the screen, occasionally moving his lips, as if talking to himself.

And Zhou Lin stood by the window, looking out at the snow. She thought of the man in Santa Monica, sitting now with his dog on his lap, drinking coffee, and perhaps smiling as he imagined this scene: five Chinese officials in a former imperial palace, trying to figure out what to do with a philosophy that's smarter than them all.

She smiled. Then she opened the folder and began reading from the first page.

Two hours later, when the snow stopped and the sun broke through the clouds, illuminating the garden and the golden patterns on the walls, Li Weilin raised his head.

— Gentlemen. I have a question for you. Not technical. Not political. Just... a question.

They looked up.

— Do you believe this will work?

Silence. Then Zhao Mingzhe said:

— Technically, yes. The architecture is functional. Prototypes exist. The evidence is publicly available.

"I'm not talking about technology," Lee interrupted softly. "I'm talking about something else. Do you believe people will agree to a system where they can't hide?"

Chen Yunhai chuckled.

"Nobody wants to answer. That's true. But you know... maybe that's exactly what we need. A system where you have to answer. Where you can't say, 'I was just following the instructions.' Where every mistake has a name. And where every victory has one, too."

"It's dangerous," General Wang said. "If people have fewer opportunities to shirk responsibility..."

"Then they'll be more careful," Zhou Lin concluded. "Or perhaps more honest. Or perhaps they'll simply understand that power and responsibility are two sides of the same sheet of paper. And that sheet can't be torn apart."

“Do you believe this?” Lee asked.

Zhou looked at the snow outside the window.

"I believe that a person who dies creates a system that will live longer than he does. And that he does this not for fame, not for money, but because... because he believes it is right. Isn't this proof that ethics exists? That it is not just words, but... action. Choice. Responsibility.

She turned to face them.

— I believe in it. Because I see it. Here, in this document, in these diagrams, in these transistors that will stop the current when it’s time to think. This is morality. Not instructions. Not laws. Not philosophical treatises. But this: a pause when you’re not sure. Refusal when you know it’s harmful. Action when you know it’s right. And a record of all this where it can’t be erased.

Li Weilin nodded.

\- Then we will work.

He took a pen—an ordinary ballpoint pen, not gold, not a gift one—and wrote on the first page of the folder:

"For study. Highest priority. December 10, 2024\. Li Weilin."

Then he closed the folder and looked at his colleagues.

"Any more questions?"

There were no questions. Or there were, but no one wanted to ask them. Because some questions don't require an answer. They require action.

Outside, the sun finally emerged from behind the clouds, and the snow covering the garden shone so brightly that for a moment it seemed as if the room had been filled with a light that had never been there. Or one that had always been there, but had simply gone unnoticed.

Zhou Lin looked at the light, then at the folder in the chairman's hands, then at her own hands lying on the table.

She thought that today, at this very moment, in Santa Monica, a man with a dog on his lap might be looking at the same snow? No, it doesn't snow in California. But maybe he's looking at the ocean. Or at his reflection in a coffee cup. Or at a dog demanding a walk.

And he thinks: “Well, let’s see what comes of this.”

She smiled and opened the folder again.

There was a lot of work.

End?

No. The beginning.
